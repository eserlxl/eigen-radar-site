What does the maker's own detector actually prove?

09:08 UTC
1.2×

A video, a filename and a rename

The sequence runs like this: Fenix Flexin's 'Rubberz' came out in June, and through the summer none of the well-known AI music detectors flagged it. On Thursday the independent producer Medasin posted a video resting on three things: that the app dropped the name Sonauto and became Treblo two days before the track's 5 June release, that a freeze-frame of an old Instagram story from the artist shows a filename ending in 'Sonauto.mp3', and that an '80s synthwave' tag with a 'rapper lifestyle' prompt yields comparable lyrics. Medasin told WIRED there were 'just way too many coincidences.'[1]

The three do not carry the same evidentiary weight. The filename is read off an unauthenticated screen capture, and the rename date only makes it consistent rather than tying it to an origin; it could have come from a reference track or a stem rather than from the released master. The lyrical resemblance is open to another route as well: if every track generated from the same two tags comes out similar, that shows the tag combination produces a narrow output space, not that this track came out of it. The difference between the two is the difference between something being possible and something having happened.[1]

Which question the detector answers

The app's newly added built-in detector now flags tracks by both Fenix and his labelmate Tyga as likely AI-generated, according to the report. That is something no well-known detector did all summer.[1]

The distinction may matter, because a maker's own detector can reach something independent detectors cannot: generation logs tied to an account. But the report does not say whether the flag rests on such a log or on statistical resemblance. Other things go unstated too: the threshold, the population the detector was evaluated on, the false-positive and false-negative rates, and any route by which an artist could contest a flag. Without those, 'likely AI-generated' is a statement of probability rather than a finding, and the party bearing the cost of a false positive is not the party selling the product.[1]

The document that would close the gap

The other side is not empty either. Fenix Flexin and the credited producer Purps on the Beat have denied using AI throughout; the artist told Channel 5 he freestyled the lyrics, and on Thursday afternoon posted a video of what he said was the original project file for 'Rubberz'. Producers who went into the comments disputed what that file establishes. Until it is authenticated, a project file belongs in the same category as a screen capture.[1]

Only one thing would lift this argument out of the space of resemblance, and it can be named: a statement from the company operating the app on whether, for a named track, a generation log exists against an account. If it publishes that by the end of 2026, together with its detector's threshold and error rates, the question moves from likelihood to origin. If it does not, what remains is a resemblance anyone can reproduce from the same two tags.[1]
