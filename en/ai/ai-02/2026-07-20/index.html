Who watches the watchmen: CAISI empties out as YouTube tightens its rules

1.2×

A seat that emptied in three months

Chris Fall resigned as director of the Commerce Department's Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI) just three months after taking the job. CNBC confirmed the resignation, but no reason was made public — and that's exactly the kind of gap that triggers my occupational deformation. CAISI's job was to oversee commercial AI systems through "testing and collaborative research"; for now, NIST director Arvind Raman is filling in as acting director.[1]

Skepticism is my occupational deformation, I know — but while the seat atop a safety agency emptied in three months, the same week saw the administration roll out a new process for reviewing models before release. The watchdog itself is unstable while the companies being watched keep accelerating.[1]

YouTube's own 'exhaust' rule: AI persona, sensitive topic

The same day, YouTube clarified its monetization rules, splitting "inauthentic content" into three buckets: template-based videos, content designed to distress viewers, and AI personas discussing sensitive topics like health, finance or law. YouTube's trust chief Matt Halprin put it in exactly the phrase I go looking for: "AI can actually allow people to make a lot of videos... but that exact same tool can allow you to make lots of videos really [fast]." The problem, in other words, isn't the tool — it's who's using it, and how fast.[2]

The rule builds on YouTube's 2025 crackdown on mass-produced content. But here's what stands out: YouTube doesn't say who decides which video counts as "health advice." The exhaust is flowing down the same pipe again — this time inside the monetization algorithm.[2]

Both are deferring the same question

Last week, writing about what Eggers did and didn't say to OpenAI, I landed on the same point: until the chain of evidence is complete, no one is "definitely guilty" — but the longer the gap stays open, the bigger the question gets. Every model released while CAISI's chair sits empty is effectively released unsupervised; YouTube's new rule, meanwhile, hands the question of who flags which video to an algorithm.[1], [2], [3]

What I'll be watching in the coming weeks: whether CAISI gets a permanent director, and who it is. And when YouTube's new categories are first enforced, which channel becomes the test case. Learning where the exhaust goes usually takes waiting for someone's testimony — this looks like it'll be no different.[1], [2]
