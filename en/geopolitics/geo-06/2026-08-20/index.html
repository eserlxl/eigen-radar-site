When Damascus weakens, its north becomes someone else's argument

01:43 UTC
1.2×

A strike, and the call that came before it

Eight Israeli strikes hit the runway of the Abu Duhur airbase in Idlib, and Netanyahu's office said Syria had been on the verge of breaching an agreed security status quo by letting Turkish troops deploy there. Damascus called the raid an unjustified violation of its sovereignty. The target was an airfield that Turkey had been helping to rebuild, well behind any front line.[1]

Four days earlier, according to Reuters, Israel's Mossad chief had phoned Syria's foreign minister to press about Turkey's growing military role — one of the highest-level Israeli-Syrian contacts since Assad fell in 2024. The quiet channel did not hold: Netanyahu said afterward that he had warned Ankara plainly, Turkey rejected the accusation, and the US tried to stand up a deconfliction mechanism.[2]

An old pattern beneath the new names

There is a long rhythm here. Whenever central authority in Damascus has faltered, outside powers have reached into Syria's northern marches and settled their rivalries on its soil. The interwar decades, when European mandates drew the region's lines, are the sharpest echo; that was also when a weakened Damascus lost the sancak of Alexandretta to Ankara. The actors change; the temptation to treat Syria's north as a place where other states resolve their fears has proved remarkably durable.[1], [2]

Read this way, the strike is less a bolt from the blue than the predictable consequence of a vacuum: Assad's fall removed the strong center that had kept outside armies at arm's length, and Turkey moved into the opening while Israel pushed back. A narrower reading is worth stating too — that this is simply Israeli deterrence math about one airbase, with history supplying color rather than cause. Both can be partly true, yet the pattern suggests the real prize is the vacuum itself rather than the runway.[1]

The difference this time

The decisive difference from the mandate era is that Damascus now acts as a player in its own right, where once it was a parcel to be assigned. Its new government has courted Washington, sought a security understanding with Israel, and leaned on Turkey — bargaining on several fronts at once rather than waiting for outsiders to fix its borders. That agency is why the quiet call mattered: an attempt to manage the rivalry by talk before it was managed by force.[1], [2]

What to watch is whether the US effort to build a deconfliction channel among Israel, Turkey and Syria takes hold, or whether each strike hardens the north into rival spheres of influence. If the US talking mechanism produces even a modest, observed pause in strikes and troop moves over the coming weeks, the vacuum is being managed; if not, the older pattern — outside powers settling accounts on Syrian ground — will keep writing the next chapter.[1], [2]
