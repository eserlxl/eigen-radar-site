Hormuz pressure moves from blockade to ship strikes and oil on Oman's coast

The UAE blamed Iran for strikes on two ADNOC vessels, Washington gave no end date for its blockade, and leaking oil reaching Oman's coast added an environmental cost to the maritime crisis.

Geopolitics·August 14, 2026·Midday
08:30 UTC

The UAE points to Iran after ADNOC ships are hit in Hormuz

Abu Dhabi's foreign ministry called Thursday evening's attack on two ADNOC vessels in the Strait of Hormuz a hostile Iranian act and described it as piracy. ADNOC confirmed the attack through the state agency WAM without naming an attacker, and reported that no one was hurt. Iran has not responded. The ministry said the Revolutionary Guard was using the strait as a tool of economic pressure and treated the strikes as a direct threat to regional stability and global energy security. The company said the incident was the second involving its ships in under a week, and that its vessels have been attacked repeatedly since the conflict began in February. Independent confirmation of who carried out the attack is not available, so the attribution remains a UAE allegation rather than an established finding. The safety of Emirati tankers that move cargo through Hormuz is therefore being read both as a local port-security issue and as a question about the strait's role as a global energy corridor.[1]

Washington sets no end date on the blockade

The same corridor is already under heavy military and commercial strain. US Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth told reporters in Panama that the navy can keep the blockade of Iranian ports going indefinitely by rotating ships in and out, and that Tehran knows there is no timeline on it. Hegseth said the pressure will continue until Iran can no longer reach a nuclear weapon, and he refused to say whether April's ceasefire had been a mistake in hindsight. Talks to end the conflict that began in February have produced no deal. Commercial traffic through the Strait of Hormuz has collapsed since the fighting began, and economists warn that the longer the disruption lasts, the more it will drag on global growth. Ship strikes on ADNOC vessels and an open-ended US blockade therefore sit inside one maritime pressure system rather than as isolated episodes. Describing the blockade as sustainable through ship rotation also places every new vessel incident in Hormuz inside a campaign that has no published calendar.[2]

Leaking oil and salvage work on Oman's coast

A separate tanker emergency off Oman has turned that system into an environmental crisis as well. Salvage work to stabilise the Caroline Bezengi, which carries sanctioned Russian crude and has leaked since an explosion in June, began on Thursday under the coordination of the British risk firm Ambrey. Omani authorities had confirmed a day earlier that oil had reached beaches on the central coast. Greenpeace put the slick at about 1,300 square kilometres on Wednesday, while Omani authorities estimated about 400 square kilometres on Monday; in late July it was around 45 square kilometres. Ambrey's director of global response, Ed Wollaston, said the work is running around the clock and that the Khareef monsoon has made conditions extremely difficult. The tanker lies off a protected area near the Hallaniyat islands, so the leak now links energy security, wartime shipping risk, and coastal damage in one stretch of water. Placing alleged strikes in Hormuz, an open-ended port blockade, and oil on Omani beaches side by side means pressure around the strait is no longer read only through transit costs or hull insurance, but also through shorelines and marine life.[1], [2], [3]
