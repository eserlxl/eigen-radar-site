Open the Model: What Pix and Stablecoins Actually Do to Visa's Moat

1.2×

Follow the Fee, Not the Flag

Strip the politics and look at the line items. Brazil's Pix is free for individuals and caps merchant fees; it processed roughly 7 billion transactions worth R$3 trillion ($590 billion) in June alone, more than double card volume, reaching 170 million people. The USTR's argument — that this "unfairly disadvantages" US payment firms — is really an admission that Visa and Mastercard's take rate depends on Pix not existing at this scale.[1]

Open the model on that admission: a 25% tariff on Brazilian goods is a blunt instrument for a precise problem — a domestic payment rail that undercuts a fee structure built for a pre-instant-payments world. You don't fix a margin compression with a tariff schedule; you fix it by building a product people choose over the free one, which is a much harder quarter to report.[1]

The Footnote Nobody's Pricing: Stablecoins Already Won the Crypto Side

Here's the sensitivity that moves the whole thesis: dollar-linked stablecoins already make up about 90% of Brazil's $6-8 billion in monthly crypto volume. Brazil's central bank is trying to wall that off from cross-border payments via Resolution 561, worried about monetary sovereignty — but that's a defensive move against something that's already the market's revealed preference.[1]

My read forward: the durable disruption to payment-network economics won't come from Washington's tariff, which is a political instrument with a shelf life tied to this administration. It will come from the compounding of Pix-style domestic rails and dollar stablecoins eating the same margin from two directions at once. Visa and Mastercard's moat was distribution; both alternatives just proved distribution is no longer the constraint.[1]
