In the US, the grocery basket and the tariff-refund door move on different tracks

PolitiFact reports prices up 4.3 per cent since January 2025 while the government asks the Federal Circuit to limit tariff refunds to firms that sued, so households and importers face different pressures.

Economics & Markets·August 12, 2026·Morning
01:03 UTC

What is rising and what is cheaper on the grocery receipt?

The PolitiFact check published by WRAL rated as Mostly False President Donald Trump's 4 August statement on Fox News that costs, food and groceries were all coming down. According to the review, US prices have risen 4.3 per cent since January 2025. Grocery prices rose 3.4 per cent over the same span, under the overall rise, while airfare is up 12.5 per cent, electricity 8 per cent, and housing and clothing 5 per cent each. On energy the picture was sharper: petrol has risen 34 per cent since January 2025, heating oil 24 per cent, natural gas 12 per cent and electricity 8 per cent, after the Iran war began on 28 February. Inside groceries, coffee is up 35 per cent, ground beef 23 per cent, steak 21 per cent and chicken breast 5.3 per cent, while bread, bacon and eggs cost less. PolitiFact noted that inflation exceeded wage growth in May for the first time this term, before wages edged ahead again in June. For households, the question is less one overall price level than which items weigh on the receipt and when wage growth catches that basket.[1]

Who still has a path to a tariff refund?

On a separate track the same day, AOL reports the reach of tariff refunds. A Court of International Trade judge had directed Customs and Border Protection to refund duties ruled illegal in February to every affected importer, including those that did not sue. In its opening brief to the Federal Circuit, the government argues that such universal relief cannot be squared with the Supreme Court's CASA ruling on nationwide injunctions, and it asks the court to limit refunds to companies that sued. So far, Customs and Border Protection has processed and certified 100 billion dollars of refunds. The dispute now covers importers whose customs entries are final and for whom the administrative refund route is closed; the government says those companies remain free to file their own suits within the applicable deadlines. A law professor quoted in the report said the government has the better legal argument, but that the approach would burden small businesses that cannot afford litigation. After the 100 billion dollars already processed, the refund fight becomes a threshold between firms that can reach a courtroom and those that cannot.[2]

Two doors, one reading

Setting these two developments side by side does not invent one inflation story; the shared point is who passes which door. The basket PolitiFact reports shows sharp rises in petrol and coffee together with declines in bread and eggs, while wages falling behind in May and leading again in June show budgets shifting month by month. The Federal Circuit fight AOL reports tries to split refunds from duties ruled illegal in February between importers that sued and those that did not; the 100 billion dollars already processed shows how far the administrative track has gone, and the remaining dispute shows whom a courtroom threshold could leave outside. For the reader the unifying question is this: while price pressure shows up on the grocery receipt and energy bill, whose favour is the tariff-refund path narrowing toward on appeal. Neither source offers investment advice; one tests a political claim against price data, the other ties refund scope to a legal boundary. The shared metaphor is two doors: cost through the household basket, and whether an importer can still pass through the refund door. This reading does not lean on a South African labour-force line; it holds the US price claim and the tariff-refund case as two faces of one pressure climate.[1], [2]
