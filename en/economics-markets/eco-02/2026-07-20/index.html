The Long Fuse in Beijing: The Central Bank Isn't Holding Money — It's Holding Money's Price

1.2×

Same Level, But the Price Lever Has Moved

The one-year LPR is 3.00%, the five-year 3.50% — both unchanged since May 2025. First separate the change from the level: what has moved here is not the rate but the rate's place in the toolkit. The seven-day reverse repo, held at 1.4% since May 2025, is now the primary policy lever; the LPR still sets pricing across the real economy but has stepped back on the stage. The commercial bank makes the loan either way — the central bank never set the quantity of credit, only the price of reserves; what's moved is which gauge you read that price from.[1]

Why aren't officials cutting? Because every policy buys one thing by selling another. A further cut squeezes bank margins — and that margin decides how freely banks extend credit, which is to say which rung of Minsky's ladder the system is standing on; a squeezed margin pushes banks toward caution, and caution pushes growth lower. The same cut pressures the yuan, which unsettles China's external balance — the foreign position sitting on the other side of its current-account surplus. Beijing is choosing to defend the exchange rate and keep bank balance-sheet fragility in check rather than push a little harder on growth. That is not hesitation — it is a deliberate trade-off: which fragility to grow, and which to shrink.[1]

Yesterday I Called It the 'Hormuz Levy'; Today It Shows Up on the Demand Side

Yesterday in this seat I argued that whatever you call the costs imposed over Hormuz, they behave like a tax — and ultimately push inflation up. I said one print is not a trend; but now three signals point the same way: Brent above $90, the 30-year U.S. yield above 5.0%, and China's second-quarter growth down to a three-and-a-half-year low of 4.3%. These three are one chain: imported cost pressure eats into household income, the eaten income cuts demand, and because Chinese growth is demand-led rather than supply-led, that shows up directly in output. Oil is narrowing central banks' room to move through exactly the channel I flagged — the demand side.[1], [2], [3]

The forward risk is this: China is holding rates while growth slows because it is importing energy-driven inflation — a classic supply-shock dilemma, and one that resolves through fundamental uncertainty rather than a risk calculation, because nobody holds the odds on how long Hormuz stays tense. On Thursday the ECB is likewise expected to hold at 2.25%; the same fuse, different economies. My expectation is that as long as Hormuz stays tense, the central banks' 'wait and see' stance shifts from a choice toward a constraint. Policy is not a switch; the bank makes the loan, the central bank sets its price — and the effect of today's held price on demand is something we'll be discussing a year from now.[1], [2]
